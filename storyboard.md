## 3. Where the maths we know runs out

| # | Narration | Visual |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3.1 | What's the speed of the car at time t equals 4 seconds? Speed is **change in distance over change in time**, or rise over run. | Speedometer shrinks back. A vertical dashed line at t = 4 on the graph; a dot where it meets the curve. Small label: rise / run. |
| 3.2 | But how can we choose two points on this graph to measure that change? The line through them vanishes. The rise and run labels count down to 0 m and 0 s, and the fraction 0 / 0 appears in red, then gives a short shake. |
| 3.4 | And it's worth noticing what went wrong here, because it's where "new maths" earns its spot in our textbooks. A different car's curve, with a section in the middle that is a straight line, highlighted. Camera zooms into that straight section until it fills the axes. |
| 3.5 | Rise over run becomes a trivial problem because I can choose any two points and I get the same answer for what the speed was. | 3.6 | The trouble starts when the **rate of change itself keeps changing**, because then the answer depends on which two points we pick. | Zoom back out to our car's curve. A gradient triangle slides along it; its value changes continuously as it moves. |
| 3.7 | This is precisely the problem that calculus was invented to solve. ## 4. Small intervals

| # | Narration | Visual |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4.1 | Going back to the car speedometer for a second now: can we draw some inspiration from the real-life engineering of this speedometer? | Terms clear. Speedometer inset returns, large. |
| 4.2 | After all, most speedometers conceptually work the same: they receive data from sensors which measure how many times the wheels turn in a short window of time. | Cutaway: a wheel turning, with a turn count beside it that keeps rising. Nothing else on screen. |
| 4.3 | So with our t equals four seconds point set, let's take a very small interval, say a tenth of a second later. | Back to the graph at t = 4. Camera zooms in close around the point. A second dot appears at t = 4.1; a bracket on the time axis between them labelled 0.1 s. |
| 4.4 | Suppose the car has travelled 80 metres by the first point and 84.05 metres by the second point. | Horizontal guide lines from each dot to the distance axis, labelled 80 m and 84.05 m. A small vertical bracket between them labelled 4.05 m. |
| 4.5 | Rise over run gives us 4.05 metres divided by 0.1 seconds, which is 40.5 metres per second. | The two bracket labels fly into a fraction: 4.05 m / 0.1 s = 40.5 m/s. A gold outline traces once around 40.5 m/s. |
| 4.6 | The straight line through these two points is called a **secant** - a word you may have met in circle geometry - and its gradient is the average speed of the car in this small window of time. | A dotted line draws through both dots, over the curve, and extends past them. The label "secant" appears, then fades soon after the word is said. |
| 4.7 | This works just like the speedometer, and is a much better answer than the average speed of 25 m/s. But it's still an average, and it still depends on the window we choose: if we take a hundredth of a second instead, we get 40.05 metres per second. This is where the new maths begins. | The 25 m/s value from part 2 appears faded beside 40.5 for comparison. Then the second dot slides from 4.1 to 4.01; the secant tilts slightly and the value updates to 40.05 m/s. |
| 4.8 | If a small window is a good idea, and a smaller window is an even better idea, then let's not pick a window size at all. Let's **postpone** that decision as much as possible, and call the length of that window h, and let's see what happens when h gets smaller and smaller. | The numeric bracket label on the time axis (0.01 s) morphs into the letter h. The second dot is relabelled t + h. |
| 4.9 | A lot of maths is just this, by the way: being stubborn and using a letter instead of a value for as long as possible. | Hold on the letter h, highlighted. No other movement. |
| 4.10 | Let's say that while the car is speeding up, its distance after t seconds is given by the function s(t) = 5t squared. | Graph slides left; the right half clears for algebra. s(t) = 5t² written top right. The first half of the curve (0 to 5 s) highlights to show where the formula applies. |
| 4.11 | After four seconds, the distance is 80 metres, and after 4.1 seconds it's 84.05 metres. | s(4) = 80 and s(4.1) = 84.05 write out under the formula. The 80 m and 84.05 m labels on the graph flash to connect them. |
| 4.12 | The average speed over a window of length h starting at time t is rise over run. So: the distance at the end of the window, which is 5(t + h) squared, minus the distance at the start, which is 5t squared, all divided by h, the length of the interval. | Numbers clear. The two dots relabel s(t) and s(t + h). The fraction builds piece by piece as each part is spoken: top 5(t + h)², then − 5t², then the bar, then h underneath. |
| 4.13 | With a little bit of **binomial expansion** here, the top becomes 5t squared plus 10th plus 5h squared, minus 5t squared. | A small side note appears: the label "binomial expansion" with (t + h)² = t² + 2th + h². The numerator transforms into 5t² + 10th + 5h² − 5t². As the new top is read out, each term lights up gold in turn: 10th, 5h², − 5t². |
| 4.14 | The 5t squared cancels out, and it makes sense that it does. | Both 5t² terms highlight in the same colour, and a dotted line links the first one to the s(t) label on the graph. Both strike through and fade. |
| 4.15 | The average speed of a window shouldn't depend on where the car was when the window opened. | On the road at the top: a faded car at t + h ahead of the car at t. The stretch of road before the car's position greys out; only the stretch covered inside the window stays bright. |
| 4.17 | Here we are allowed to simplify the fraction by h, because, remember, h itself is a real, non-zero window length. | The h in each top term highlights in the window colour, matching the h bracket on the graph. Those h's and the h underneath strike out together. The bracket on the graph stays visibly open, not collapsed. |
| 4.18 | What remains after the cancellation is 10t + 5h. | Result: 10t + 5h, boxed. |
| 4.19 | And what is this, really? What we arrived at is a function that tells us the average speed of the car over a window that starts at time t and lasts h seconds, where h is just a length that we haven't chosen yet. | The boxed 10t + 5h and the secant on the graph highlight together, then the h bracket highlights. |
| 4.21 | At four seconds, the average over the window is 40 + 5h. A window of a tenth of a second gives 40.5 m/s. A hundredth of a second gives 40.05. A thousandth gives 40.005 m/s, and so on. | t = 4 substitutes in: 40 + 5h. A small table builds row by row (h = 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 against 40.5, 40.05, 40.005), its column headed "window after". In sync on the zoomed graph, the second dot moves closer each row and the secant tilts. |
| 4.22 | Now, the curious among us might also check intervals before our t equals four seconds point, just to see what happens. | On the zoomed graph, a second outer dot appears at t = 4 − h, to the left of the point, with a second dotted secant through it and (4, 80). 40 − 5h appears beside 40 + 5h, and the table gains a left column headed "before" (39.5, 39.95, 39.995). Both outer dots step inwards together as the column fills. |
| 4.23 | Since the car is speeding up the whole time, its speed at the instant must sit between the average just before and the average just after. | Below the table, a number line: the before-average marked from the left, the after-average from the right, and the stretch between them shaded. The shaded stretch narrows row by row around 40. |
| 4.24 | There is exactly **one number** that stays between the two columns on every row, however far down you go, and that number is 40. Try any other number, say 40.001, and we can find a window small enough, say a ten-thousandth of a second, for which the average speed is 40.0005, no longer trapping our guess. | The number line zooms in around 40. A marker at 40.001 sits inside the shaded stretch for the first three rows. A fourth row appears (h = 0.0001: 39.9995 and 40.0005), the stretch narrows past the marker, and it is left outside, shakes and fades. The marker at 40 stays inside throughout. When "forty" is said, its marker gives a small green burst. |
| 4.25 | So finally, we have arrived at the definition that we were missing: the speed at an instant is **the number the averages close in on, from both sides, as the window becomes smaller and smaller**. For our car at four seconds, that number is 40 m/s. | The question from 2.9 returns: "speed at t = 4 s = ?" and the ? becomes 40 m/s. The red 0 / 0 from 3.3 appears briefly beside it, then strikes out. Speedometer inset needle settles on 40. |
| 4.26 | Mathematicians have a name for this kind of number - the number a quantity closes in on as something else shrinks to zero - and it's called a **limit**. It's written like this: "lim", with "h → 0" underneath, in front of the expression. We read it as "the limit, as h approaches zero, of 40 + 5h", and it means the one number that this expression gets as close as we like to, from either side, as h shrinks, without h ever being zero. | The notation assembles: "lim" appears, "h → 0" drops in underneath, then 40 + 5h slides in to its right, and "= 40" completes it. Each piece highlights as the narration reads it. Under the 0, a small arrow from each side shows h arriving from both directions. |
| 4.27 | And if we don't plug in four seconds but keep t in this expression, the same reasoning turns 10t + 5h into 10t. | Back to the boxed 10t + 5h. The 5h term dims and slides away as an arrow labelled "h → 0" points to the result: 10t. |
| 4.28 | Observe the same thing graphically now. And as h gets smaller and smaller, our two secants get closer and closer until they settle on one particular line. That line is the **tangent** to the curve at four seconds. | Algebra side dims. Full-width graph. Both outer dots slide continuously towards t = 4; the two secants rotate towards each other, pivoting on (4, 80), the angle between them shrinking until they merge into one line. The label "secants" becomes "tangent". The line is dotted and green, same green we use for the number 40 previously. As the tangent lands, a small green burst at (4, 80). |
| 4.29 | As we might expect, the gradient of the tangent is the speed at that instant. | A gradient triangle on the tangent labelled 40. The speedometer inset reads 40. |
| 4.30 | But now let's step back and look at what we actually achieved. We wrote rise over run for a window of length h, we refused to choose h, we did the algebra, and then we asked where the answer heads as h shrinks to zero from both sides. Written out in general, for any function s(t), the whole procedure is this: | The number 4 in every label on screen morphs back into t. The full definition assembles: s′(t) = lim h→0 \[s(t + h) − s(t)\] / h. |
| 4.31 | *T**his* is a **derivative**, and the act of taking the limit of rise over run is called **differentiating** the function.  We read s dash of t as "the derivative of s". | Label "derivative" under the formula. Formula moves to the top of the screen, smaller, and stays for the rest of part 4. |
| 4.32 | One more seemingly minor thing to notice but worth pointing out: the derivative of 5 times t squared is itself also a function of t, namely 10 times t. It lets us find the speed at every moment of the trip - 10 metres per second after one second, 20 after two, 50 after five. | Beside the distance-time graph, a speed-time graph draws for the whole trip: a triangle rising to 50 m/s at 5 s (v = 10t), then back down to 0 at 10 s. A dot on each graph moves to t = 1, 2 and 5, with a short tangent on the distance curve and the speed read off the bottom graph (10, 20, 50). |
| 4.33 | And if you've seen a textbook rule for derivatives, you've already met this result. The rule says: bring the power down in front, and knock one off the power. 5t squared becomes 2 times 5 times t, which is 10t, exactly what the limit gave us. | 5t² on screen. The 2 arcs down to the front and the exponent drops to 1: 2 × 5 × t¹. The result 10t slides next to the boxed 10t from the limit and the two match. |

## 5. Doing it twice

| # | Narration | Visual |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5.1 | Since the derivative itself is also a function, does that mean that we can also say something about its rate of change? | The distance-time graph and the speed-time graph from 4.32 move into a stack on the left, both covering the whole 10 s trip, with an arrow between them. Empty axes for a third graph appear below, with a second arrow. |
| 5.2 | Turns out we can. Running the same procedure on 10t, the average change over a window of length h is 10(t + h) minus 10t, over h, which is just 10. | Quick algebra on the right in small type: \[10(t + h) − 10t\] / h → 10h / h → 10. On the speed graph, a secant with an h bracket; it already lies on the line, so shrinking h changes nothing. |
| 5.3 | So the speed goes up by 10 metres per second every second, and what comes out of **differentiating twice** is the car's **acceleration**. | A speedometer ticks up in even steps, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, one per second, then runs back down to 0 as the car brakes. The third graph draws in step: a flat line at +10 m/s² for the first 5 s, then −10 m/s² for the braking half. A brace across both arrows reads "differentiating twice"; the third graph is titled "acceleration". |
| 5.6 | But differentiation produces meaningful functions and numbers in many domains. Take the total number of **cases in an epidemic**. Its derivative is **new cases per day**, and the derivative of that tells you **whether the outbreak is speeding up** or slowing down. | The three graphs morph into a new stack with the same arrows: total cases (an S-curve), new cases per day (a hump), change in new cases per day (positive, then crossing zero, then negative). |
| 5.7 | When we were hearing news about the curve flattening in 2020, what they meant was that we were still getting new cases every day, just at a decreasing rate compared to the day before. | A vertical cursor at the peak of the hump. On the bottom graph the same cursor sits where the line crosses zero. On the top graph total cases are still rising there. A news-style upper-third caption: "the curve is flattening". |
| 5.9 | Whenever you ask **how fast something is changing**, you're asking for a **derivative**; whenever you ask whether that **change is speeding up **or slowing down, you're asking for the **derivative of a derivative**. | The car and epidemic AND current stacks appear side by side as small thumbnails, aligned so their arrows line up. We still show the charge -> current -> rate of change here even if we dont mention it in the script.
