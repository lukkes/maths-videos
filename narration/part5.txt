Since the derivative is itself a function, does that mean we can also say something about its rate of change?

Turns out we can! Running the same procedure on 10t, the average change over a window of length h is 10(t + h) minus 10t, over h, which is just 10.

So the speed goes up by 10 metres per second, every second, and what came out of differentiating twice is the car's acceleration.

But differentiation produces meaningful functions and numbers in many domains. Take the total number of cases in an epidemic: its derivative is new cases per day, and the derivative of that tells you whether the outbreak is speeding up or slowing down.

When we were hearing news about the curve flattening in 2020, what they meant was that we were still getting new cases every day, just at a decreasing rate compared to the day before.

Or take the charge that has flowed through a wire: its derivative is the current, and the rate at which the current changes is what produces a voltage across a coil, which is how the ignition coil in a petrol engine turns a small current into a spark.

Whenever you ask how fast something is changing, you're asking for a derivative; whenever you ask whether that change is speeding up or slowing down, you're asking for the derivative of a derivative.
