Going back to the car speedometer for a second now: can we draw some inspiration from the real-life engineering of this?

After all, most speedometers conceptually work the same: they receive data from sensors which measure how many times the wheels turn in a short window of time.

So with our t equals four seconds point set, let's take a very small interval, say a tenth of a second later.

Suppose the car has travelled 80 metres by the first point and 84.05 metres by the second.

Rise over run gives us 4.05 metres divided by 0.1 seconds, which is 40.5 metres per second.

The straight line through these two points is called a secant - a word you may have met in circle geometry - and its gradient is the average speed of the car in this small window of time. Just like the speedometer's.

This is a much better answer than the average speed of 25 m/s. But it's still an average, and it still depends on the window we chose: if we take a hundredth of a second instead, we get 40.05 metres per second.

This is where the new maths begins. If a small window is a good idea, and a smaller window is an even better idea, then let's not pick a window size at all. Let's postpone that decision as much as possible, and call the length of that window h, and let's see what happens when h gets smaller and smaller.

A lot of maths is just this: being stubborn and using a letter instead of a value for as long as possible.

Let's say that while the car is speeding up, its distance after t seconds is given by the function s(t) = 5t squared metres. This is just an example to help us work with more concrete quantities, but keep in mind that this function could be anything.

After 4 seconds, the distance is five times 16, which is 80 metres, and after 4.1 seconds it's five times 16.81, which is 84.05 metres.

The average speed over a window of length h starting at time t is rise over run: the distance at the end of the window, which is 5(t+h) squared, minus the distance at the start which is 5t squared, all divided by h, the length of the interval.

With a little bit of binomial expansion - (t + h) squared is t squared plus 2th plus h squared - the top becomes 5t squared plus 10th plus 5h squared, minus 5t squared.

The 5t squared cancels out, and if we think a little bit about it, it makes sense that it does. That quantity represents a distance, namely where the car was at the start of our window.

The average speed over a window shouldn't depend on where the car was when the window opened.

What survives is every term with at least one h in it, which makes sense too: covering any distance takes some time, so every bit of movement inside the window carries an h with it.

Here we are allowed to simplify the fraction by h because: remember that h itself is a real, non-zero window length.

What remains after that cancellation is 10t + 5h.

What is this, really? What we arrived at is a function that tells us the average speed of the car over a window that starts at time t and lasts h seconds, where h is just a length we haven't chosen yet.

Notice the shape of it: 10t, which doesn't involve the window at all, plus 5h, a correction that is only there because the window has some length and the car speeds up a little during it.

At four seconds, the average over the window is 40 + 5h. A window of a tenth of a second gives 40.5 m/s. A hundredth of a second gives 40.05, a thousandth gives 40.005 m/s, and so on.
